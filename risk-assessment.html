Information Security Beyond Computers: Lessons from Our Risk Assessment Fieldwork

A reflection on information security in practice within a local retail organization

By John Ryan Wong Paler 2026

IN THIS ARTICLE

When people hear the term information security, they often think immediately of computers, passwords, hackers, antivirus software, and large corporate networks. Before conducting our group’s risk assessment fieldwork, we also tended to associate information security mainly with digital technology.

However, our experience assessing a local retail and sales establishment showed us that information security is much broader. Information can exist in notebooks, mobile phones, payment applications, customer records, supplier records, cash-handling processes, and even in the daily habits of workers.

Our fieldwork allowed us to observe how information security concepts apply in a real working environment. To protect the confidentiality of the organization, this reflection discusses the establishment only in general terms and does not identify its owner, workers, exact location, or other details that could expose the organization.

Identifying Assets Before Identifying Risks

One of the first lessons we applied from our module was that we could not properly assess risk without first identifying the assets that were important to the organization.

During the assessment, we identified assets such as daily sales records, customer information, supplier information, cash and sales revenue, electronic payment accounts, business-related mobile devices, physical records, monitoring equipment, and inventory.

This helped us understand that an asset is not limited to hardware. Information itself can be valuable.

Risk Is About More Than the Threat

Another important lesson was learning to distinguish between an asset, a risk source, and a risk scenario.

Simply writing “theft” was not enough for our assessment. Theft is a possible source of risk, but we also had to describe what could be affected and what the consequence could be.

Existing Controls Matter

Another important part of our assessment was identifying controls that were already being used.

Evaluating Impact and Probability

The Risk Assessment Form required us to evaluate both the impact and probability of each identified risk.

Accepting Risk Is Also a Decision

Before doing this activity, we assumed that every identified risk had to be eliminated. The fieldwork taught us that this is not always realistic.

Recommending Practical Controls

Our recommendations focused on controls that were realistic for the type and size of the organization.

The Human Element of Information Security

Our fieldwork also showed us how important people are in information security.

Professional Conduct and Confidentiality

Another major lesson from this activity was that risk assessment itself carries responsibilities.

Conclusion

Our risk assessment fieldwork changed our understanding of information security from a mainly technical subject into a practical business concern.
