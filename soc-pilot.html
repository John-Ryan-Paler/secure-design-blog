Can AI Really Fly the SOC?

Why the future of autonomous security operations still needs human pilots, trustworthy context, and clear limits.

By John Ryan Wong PalerOctober 2026Critical reflection

IN THIS ARTICLE

My position: A SOC pilot model is a realistic direction for cybersecurity, but only when AI autonomy is limited by verified asset context, tested safeguards, and meaningful human authority.

A SOC on Autopilot?

Imagine a security operations center (SOC) in which analysts do not spend every shift opening repetitive alerts, copying IP addresses between tools, and deciding whether another familiar detection is a false positive. Instead, software agents gather evidence, investigate suspicious activity, and perform approved responses. Human analysts focus on unusual incidents and decisions that may seriously affect the organization. This is the future described by John Velisaris in IBM's article, How agentic AI enables an autonomous SOC with minimal human involvement (2025).

Velisaris compares the future SOC to a modern airliner: machines perform much of the routine work while pilots intervene when the situation requires expertise. He calls the human analysts “SOC pilots” (Velisaris, 2025). The comparison is appealing because cybersecurity teams face alert fatigue, fragmented tools, and pressure to respond faster than attackers. But it also raises a difficult question: when an AI system can take action rather than merely recommend it, who ensures that its decisions are safe?

I think the SOC pilot idea can work for certain tasks. However, I do not think it is safe to let AI handle everything without people being responsible for its decisions. An agent may process evidence faster than an analyst, yet speed cannot compensate for incorrect information or a harmful response. The realistic goal is not a SOC without people. It is a SOC in which people spend less time repeating predictable tasks and more time supervising high-impact decisions.

How Realistic Is the Pilot Model?

IBM's argument is strongest when it distinguishes simple automation from autonomy. Traditional security orchestration, automation, and response (SOAR) playbooks follow predefined conditions. An agentic system can go further: identify what information is missing, request it from connected tools, interpret the results, and choose an appropriate workflow. Velisaris (2025) describes IBM's autonomous threat operations machine, ATOM, coordinating agents that consult vulnerability-management tools, configuration management databases (CMDBs), and endpoint detection and response (EDR) systems.

This makes more sense to me than expecting one AI chatbot to protect an entire company. Security work already depends on combining multiple sources, and software can reduce the delay caused by manually switching between dashboards. A well-integrated environment with accurate inventory records and carefully defined response procedures could allow agents to handle many routine investigations. IBM also notes that extensive automation is easier in closed systems whose assets and behavior are well understood (Velisaris, 2025).

However, most organizations are not closed systems. They have legacy servers, personal devices, cloud services, temporary accounts, contractors, and incomplete inventories. New attacks may deliberately resemble normal operations. Agents may also produce confident but mistaken interpretations of evidence. The airline analogy therefore has limits: a SOC does not operate in a fixed environment, and attackers intentionally manipulate what defenders can observe. Importantly, Velisaris (2025) acknowledges that the complete move to the SOC pilot model remains uncertain. I see it as a gradual operational transformation, not a finished capability that every organization can safely purchase today.

Where AI Can Safely Act on Its Own

AI should receive the greatest freedom in tasks that are repetitive, reversible, observable, and low impact. For example, an agent can collect logs from several tools, enrich an alert with threat intelligence, compare a file hash with known indicators, summarize related events, or open a ticket containing the evidence. These tasks save analysts time without necessarily changing production systems. Agents can also group duplicate alerts and recommend priorities, provided the underlying evidence remains available for review.

Some containment actions may be suitable for automatic execution, but only within tightly defined boundaries. Consider a managed employee workstation that is positively identified, shows a confirmed malicious process, and is covered by an approved isolation policy. An EDR tool might temporarily isolate it, record the reason, and notify the SOC. Even then, the organization should verify the detection quality, document exceptions, allow rapid rollback, and ensure that isolation will not interrupt essential operations.

AI should not be given complete control all at once. A useful policy might allow automatic evidence gathering at any time, automatic containment only for verified low-criticality assets under high-confidence conditions, and human approval for destructive or business-critical actions. It is also important to distinguish confidence in a malicious indicator from confidence in the identity of the affected asset. Both matter. An agent that recognizes malware correctly can still make a dangerous decision about where to intervene.

Where Human Judgment Must Remain

People still need to make the final call when the evidence is unclear, the risk is serious, or a decision could affect how the organization operates. A zero-day attack is an obvious example. A novel exploit may not match familiar patterns, and the response could require creative investigation, new detection rules, coordination with vendors, and a judgment about whether to interrupt business operations. IBM specifically identifies such uncertainty as a moment for human SOC pilots to take command (Velisaris, 2025).

Other decisions also demand human authority: shutting down a production database, blocking a major business partner, disabling a privileged executive account, restoring compromised systems, or deciding whether a suspected breach requires external notification. A technically effective containment step may cause more immediate damage than the threat it stops. Analysts need to weigh confidentiality, integrity, availability, safety, legal obligations, and business continuity together.

Analysts also need to question the AI instead of automatically trusting it. If an agent presents a neat summary that leaves out contradictory logs, an analyst should be able to inspect the original evidence and reject its conclusion. Oversight cannot mean clicking “approve” without understanding the action. It requires the power to pause automation, request a second investigation, override a response, and document why the decision changed. Responsibility remains with the organization and its authorized professionals, not with a model that generates recommendations.

When Asset Context Is Incomplete or Wrong

For me, the most important part of the IBM article is its emphasis on context. Velisaris (2025) gives an example of a playbook that can quarantine a host if it is not a server and is performing recognized malicious activity. That condition cannot be evaluated until the system knows what the host actually is. An ordinary workstation and a critical server may produce similar security alerts, but isolating them can have completely different consequences.

Imagine an AI agent receiving an alert from a device labeled “workstation” in an outdated CMDB. In reality, the device was repurposed last month to host an essential internal application. The agent trusts the old label and automatically isolates it. The suspected attack may be contained, but employees lose access to a critical service. This is not merely a classification error; it is an operational incident caused by bad context.

The opposite error is dangerous too. A compromised device incorrectly labeled as a protected production server may be excluded from automatic containment. Malware then has more time to spread. Missing ownership, incorrect network mappings, duplicate hostnames, stale vulnerability records, and inconsistent cloud tags can all mislead an agent. Even gathering more data does not guarantee truth if several systems copied the same original mistake.

Therefore, asset information should be treated as evidence with provenance and freshness, not as unquestionable fact. Before taking a consequential action, the agent should cross-check independent sources such as endpoint telemetry, cloud inventory, network behavior, and recent change records. It should identify conflicting values, record timestamps, and make uncertainty visible. If it cannot establish whether a device is critical, the correct response is not to guess. It can continue collecting evidence, increase monitoring, alert an analyst, or request approval before isolation.

This approach may slow a response by a few minutes, but the alternative could be an avoidable outage. A responsible system should fail safely when asset identity or business criticality is uncertain. In cybersecurity, “unknown” is a meaningful status, not a blank that an AI agent should confidently fill in.

Building Responsible Autonomy

Responsible autonomy requires governance before deployment, not after the first automated mistake. The National Institute of Standards and Technology's Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework organizes AI risk management around four functions: govern, map, measure, and manage (Tabassi, 2023). Although it is not a SOC-specific rulebook, it offers a useful way to evaluate an autonomous security workflow.

Govern means assigning owners to automated actions, defining approval levels, maintaining audit logs, and deciding who can suspend the system. Map means understanding the environment, the assets affected, and the possible harm of a wrong decision. Measure means testing detection quality, false positives, asset-context accuracy, response time, and business disruption. Manage means adjusting permissions and controls as risks change. These principles align with NIST's emphasis on trustworthy, accountable, and resilient AI systems (Tabassi, 2023).

I would also require a staged rollout. First, the agent operates in observation mode and writes recommendations without executing them. Next, it handles low-risk tasks while analysts compare its decisions against established procedures. Only after repeated testing should the organization authorize narrow containment actions. Every automated action should include a reason, the evidence used, the policy permitting it, and a rollback procedure. Regular exercises should simulate missing asset records, conflicting alerts, compromised data feeds, and unexpected outages.

Finally, autonomy must have a clear stopping point. If tool connections fail, records conflict, confidence falls, or an action exceeds the approved impact threshold, the system should escalate. The goal is not to maximize the number of decisions made without humans. The goal is to reduce real security risk without introducing new, uncontrolled operational risk.

Becoming a SOC Pilot Instead of Being Replaced

As an information technology student interested in cybersecurity and secure systems, I see the SOC pilot concept as a challenge to improve my skills rather than a reason to avoid the field. If machines increasingly handle basic alert sorting, memorizing tool menus will not be enough. I will need to understand networking, operating systems, authentication, endpoint behavior, cloud infrastructure, and common attack techniques so I can recognize when an AI-generated explanation does not fit the evidence.

I also need practical incident-response skills: interpreting logs, reconstructing timelines, validating indicators, preserving evidence, and choosing containment steps based on risk. Learning scripting and automation will help me inspect what an agent actually does instead of treating it as a black box. Understanding asset inventories, CMDBs, vulnerability management, and system dependencies will be equally important because accurate context determines whether automation is safe.

Beyond technical knowledge, I must develop critical thinking, communication, and ethical judgment. A SOC pilot may need to explain to management why a server cannot safely be isolated, tell another team why an AI recommendation was rejected, or coordinate a response during uncertainty. I want to practice questioning assumptions, documenting decisions, and accepting accountability for consequences. Those abilities cannot be replaced simply by generating a faster alert summary.

Most importantly, I need to become comfortable working with AI while remaining capable of working without it. If I depend on the agent for every conclusion, I will not be prepared to take control when the system fails. A real pilot understands the aircraft and the situation; similarly, a SOC pilot must understand the security environment well enough to detect when the automation is wrong.

Conclusion: Autonomy Needs Accountability

The SOC pilot model is realistic as a direction for modern security operations, especially for routine investigation and carefully bounded response. It is not yet a responsible excuse for removing analysts from important decisions. IBM's vision demonstrates how agents can gather missing context and coordinate tools, while NIST's framework reminds us that trustworthy AI requires ongoing governance, evaluation, and risk management (Tabassi, 2023; Velisaris, 2025).

My final view is that the best autonomous SOC will not be the one that acts most often without permission. It will be the one that knows which actions are safe, recognizes when its information is incomplete, and calls on skilled humans before a mistake becomes a crisis. I would rather become the analyst who can question, guide, and improve that system than someone who merely watches its dashboard. The future SOC may have an autopilot, but responsible security still needs someone qualified to take the controls.
