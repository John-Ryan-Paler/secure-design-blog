From Risk Assessment to Audit: Are Our Security Controls Really Working?

Revisiting our group's Risk Assessment Form through the eyes of an auditor

Introduction

When our group completed a Risk Assessment Form for a small retail and sales operation, I initially thought the main challenge was identifying threats and proposing solutions. We examined what could be lost, damaged, or accessed without permission. We then considered the impact and probability of each risk and recommended practical controls. That process taught me that information security is not limited to computers. It also involves money, notebooks, customer details, business phones, physical records, monitoring equipment, and goods that support everyday operations.

Revisiting the form as an auditor changes the questions I would ask. Instead of stopping at 'What might happen?' I would ask, 'Can the business show what it owns, who is accountable for each asset, and whether the controls are actually working?' I would also distinguish between a recommended treatment and a completed, tested improvement. This reflection uses our group's documented assessment as its starting point, while keeping the establishment's identity, personnel, and operational details confidential.

What Our Form Tells Us About Assets

Our completed form contains ten risk entries covering customer credit records, cash and sales revenue, a business e-wallet account, a business cellphone, daily sales records, supplier records, customer contact information, physical business records, CCTV or physical security monitoring, and inventory or goods. Seeing these items together is useful because it shows that a business depends on both information and physical resources. Losing a notebook can affect decisions just as much as losing a digital file. A missing product or an interrupted payment process can also disrupt normal work.

Still, a risk list is not automatically a complete asset inventory. The form identifies assets relevant to the scenarios our group assessed, but it does not by itself establish that every asset has been recorded, uniquely identified, or reviewed for accuracy. An auditor would compare the form with an actual inventory, check whether business devices and important records are accounted for, and ask how additions or changes are documented. The NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 places asset identification within the Identify function, which helps organizations understand what needs protection (Pascoe et al., 2024).

Which Assets Deserve the Most Attention?

The form rates two initial risks as Significant: cash or sales revenue and the business e-wallet account. Each has a risk score of six, based on a Major impact and Possible probability. These deserve priority because financial loss or unauthorized transactions can directly affect business operations. The remaining eight completed entries are rated Moderate initially, with scores of four. This does not mean they are unimportant. Customer credit records, contact information, and daily sales records can affect trust, accuracy, and continuity if mishandled.

From an auditor's perspective, the organization should classify information according to its sensitivity and business value. Customer contact details, credit records, and payment-related information would generally need restricted handling, while internal operational records should have clear rules about who may view or update them. Goods, cash, devices, and monitoring equipment are physical assets that also need appropriate safeguards. These classifications are recommendations for a formal policy, not classifications already verified in our spreadsheet. The important step is to make the rules explicit and consistent instead of relying on assumptions.

Who Is Responsible for Each Asset?

Our Risk Assessment Form does something useful: it names responsible roles for each entry. Many treatments are assigned to the Owner / Cashier, while monitoring and inventory responsibilities include the Owner / Assigned Staff. It also records target completion periods, most commonly within one month, with ongoing monitoring for CCTV. This shows that our group did not simply write down solutions without considering who might carry them out.

However, the role labels are a starting point, not full proof of accountability. An auditor would ask whether the people in those roles understand their duties, whether responsibilities are formally communicated, and who takes over when someone is absent. For example, who confirms that daily sales entries have been reviewed? Who approves access to the e-wallet account? Who checks that inventory counts are completed? A simple asset inventory with an owner, custodian, classification, review date, and control responsibility would help make those answers consistent. Ownership should continue even when individual staff members change.

Controls on Paper Versus Controls in Practice

The existing controls recorded in our form include keeping some records in a cabinet, assigning access to the business e-wallet to the cashier, recording sales on paper, and using CCTV to monitor the business area. Our proposed additional treatments include locked storage, limited access, secure cash handling, transaction reconciliation, stronger passwords, two-factor authentication, device screen locks, backup copies, regular reviews, and inventory counts. These recommendations are practical because they connect directly to the activities of a small retail operation.

An auditor, however, cannot conclude that a control works simply because it appears in the treatment column. A recommendation to enable two-factor authentication is not evidence that it has been enabled. A plan to make backup copies does not prove that backups are complete or restorable. A suggestion to review sales records does not establish that reviews happen regularly. NIST SP 800-53A describes assessment approaches for examining, interviewing, and testing controls to determine whether they are implemented and operating as intended (Pillitteri, 2022). That difference between intention and evidence is central to this reflection.

What the Review of Effectiveness Really Shows

The form includes a Review of Effectiveness section with follow-up impact, probability, and risk scores. In nine completed entries, the reviewed score is lower than the original score. For example, the two initially Significant financial risks move from a score of six to a reviewed score of three. Most of the other entries move from four to two. The physical business records entry remains at four, meaning its recorded probability has not been reduced in the same way.

These scores are useful for showing the expected or assessed direction of risk after treatment. But the spreadsheet alone does not provide test records, supporting attachments, or signed verification showing that each proposed measure was implemented successfully. As an auditor, I would not treat the lower numbers as independent proof that the business is now secure. I would ask how the follow-up ratings were determined, what evidence supported them, when the controls were checked, and whether any corrective actions remained open. The unchanged score for physical business records would especially deserve follow-up, without assuming that a specific failure has already occurred.

What Evidence Would I Ask to See?

For cash and sales revenue, I would request general evidence of secure cash-handling procedures and periodic reconciliation, without publishing amounts or transaction details. For the business e-wallet, I would check whether access is limited to approved roles and whether the recommended authentication measures are active. For customer credit records and contact information, I would examine documented access rules, storage practices, and how records are protected when they are no longer needed.

For daily sales and supplier records, I would ask for evidence of regular review and a sample of backup or recovery checks. For the business cellphone, I would verify that screen locking and appropriate account protections are in place. For physical records, I would inspect the general storage process and look for an accountable person responsible for it. For CCTV, I would request maintenance or functionality checks, while respecting privacy. For inventory, I would examine whether periodic counts and discrepancy reviews are recorded.

Evidence should be dated, relevant, and appropriate to the control. A verbal statement can explain how a process works, but it is stronger when supported by a record or a direct observation. An audit should collect only what is necessary and protect sensitive business information during the review.

Possible Audit Gaps, Not Accusations

A formal audit could uncover gaps in asset completeness, classification, documented ownership, or evidence retention. For example, an organization might know where its important notebooks and devices are but have no updated master list. Employees might follow sensible routines without recording when checks were completed. Risk scores might be revised without a clear explanation of the supporting tests. These are reasonable questions to investigate, not confirmed findings about the organization we assessed.

Our group had a limited fieldwork perspective, and the form was designed primarily for risk identification, evaluation, and treatment. It was not a complete independent audit. I would therefore avoid describing missing evidence in the spreadsheet as proof that controls are absent in real life. Instead, I would document an evidence request, examine the available materials, and only then determine whether a gap exists. Fairness and confidentiality matter as much as technical accuracy.

How an Inventory and Checklist Would Strengthen Our Treatments

The first improvement I would recommend is a maintained asset inventory. It could be a straightforward spreadsheet containing an asset identifier, asset category, general location, classification, responsible role, related risk entry, required control, and date of last review. The ten assets already identified in our Risk Assessment Form would provide an initial starting list. Management could then verify whether anything important was omitted and update the inventory whenever equipment, records, or responsibilities change.

The second improvement is a short audit checklist linked to the same entries. Each row should contain a question, expected evidence, reviewer, review date, result, and follow-up action. For example: 'Was the cash-handling process reviewed?' 'Is e-wallet access restricted and protected?' 'Were backup copies checked?' 'Were inventory counts documented?' 'Was CCTV functionality reviewed?' 'Are physical records stored according to the approved procedure?' This turns our treatment recommendations into activities that can be verified rather than forgotten after submission.

The checklist should also track exceptions. If a control is not yet in place, the responsible role should record what remains to be done and when it will be checked again. The form records one Accept treatment for CCTV or physical security monitoring, alongside continued monitoring and basic maintenance. An auditor would want to confirm that acceptance is a conscious, authorized decision and that the ongoing activities are understood. Risk acceptance should not mean that nobody follows up.

Conclusion: From Identifying Risks to Proving Protection

This exercise changed how I look at our group's work. Our Risk Assessment Form identifies ten relevant assets, gives initial risk ratings, recommends practical treatments, assigns responsible roles, and records reviewed risk scores. That is a useful foundation. But an auditor needs to go further by checking the completeness of the asset list, confirming ownership, examining supporting evidence, and testing whether controls actually work.

The most important lesson for me is that a lower risk score is not the same as verified protection. A secure cabinet, a backup process, or a two-factor authentication requirement matters only when it is properly implemented and maintained. A simple asset inventory and evidence-based audit checklist would help the organization connect each recommendation to a responsible role, a repeatable check, and a clear record of results. As an IT student, I now see risk assessment and auditing as connected activities: one identifies what needs attention, while the other helps demonstrate whether the response is working.
